# TOProadmap

Turn "I want to learn/do X" into a clear, realistic, detailed roadmap the person can actually follow. A good roadmap answers four questions: where am I going, what are the stages, what exactly do I do in each stage, and how do I know I'm ready to move on.

## Step 1: Understand the goal (briefly)

A roadmap for a beginner with 5 hours a week is very different from one for an experienced person with a deadline, so find out:

- **The goal**: what "done" looks like (job, project, exam, fluency, a habit)
- **Current level**: complete beginner, some experience, or advanced
- **Time**: hours per week and any deadline
- **Constraints**: budget, tools, language, location, learning style

If the request already answers most of this, do not interrogate the person. Make sensible assumptions, state them in one line at the top ("Assuming you're a beginner with about 7 hrs/week"), and build the roadmap. Only ask first if the goal itself is unclear. Ask at most 3 short questions, and use the tappable-options tool if it's available.

## Step 2: Get current information when it matters

For fast-moving topics (software, tools, frameworks, AI, certifications, laws, job markets, prices, exam formats), run a web search so resources, versions, and requirements are current. For timeless topics (a musical instrument, math, cooking basics), skip the search. Never invent specific courses, books, or links; recommend only ones you are confident exist, or that you found, and otherwise describe the type of resource to look for.

## Step 3: Build the roadmap

Use this structure. Keep the order, because it moves from the big picture down to daily actions.

### 1. Snapshot
- **Goal** in one sentence
- **Assumptions** (level, time, constraints)
- **Total estimated duration** and the weekly time commitment
- **Final outcome**: what the person will be able to do at the end

### 2. The big picture
A short overview of the stages, ideally as a simple line or table: `Stage 1 → Stage 2 → Stage 3 → ...` with a duration for each. Use 4 to 6 stages for most goals. Fewer for small goals, more only for very large ones.

### 3. Stage-by-stage plan
For each stage, give:
- **Name and duration** (e.g., "Stage 2: Core Skills, Weeks 5-10")
- **Objective**: what this stage achieves and why it comes now
- **What to learn / do**: concrete topics or actions, in order
- **Resources**: specific, high-quality ones (free options first, paid ones flagged as optional)
- **Practice / mini-project**: something hands-on, not just reading or watching
- **Milestone check**: a clear test of readiness to move on ("you can explain X without notes", "you shipped Y")
- **Common mistakes** at this stage

### 4. First 7 days
A concrete day-by-day or step-by-step start, so the person can begin today. Momentum matters more than a perfect plan.

### 5. Tracking and staying on course
- How to measure progress (milestones, a weekly review question)
- What to do when stuck or falling behind
- When to adjust the plan

### 6. After the roadmap
What comes next: advanced paths, specializations, communities, or ways to use the skill (portfolio, job applications, teaching others).

## Quality rules

- **Be specific.** "Learn the basics" is useless; "Complete variables, loops, and functions, then build a number-guessing game" is useful. Every step should be something the person can start doing.
- **Be realistic.** Fit the plan to the time they actually have. Add buffer for real life. A plan they can keep beats an ambitious one they abandon.
- **Sequence for a reason.** Put prerequisites first, and explain briefly why each stage comes where it does.
- **Practice over passive study.** Each stage should include building, doing, or testing, not only consuming content.
- **Be honest.** If the goal is hard, slow, or needs money or luck (e.g., a competitive career), say so plainly and note the realistic range of timelines.
- **Match the person.** Use their language and level. Avoid jargon without a one-line explanation for beginners.
- **Stay scannable.** Use headers, short bullets, and tables where they help, but keep the writing clear and human, not bloated.

## Delivery

Write the roadmap in the chat by default. After it, offer in one line to put it in a downloadable file (Word, PDF, or a doc) or to build a tracker spreadsheet. If the person asks for a file in a specific format, create it using the matching skill.

End by offering to adjust the roadmap (more or less time, a harder or easier pace, a different focus) or to go deeper on any single stage.
